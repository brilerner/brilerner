I had a wonderful time at CHIL 2026, the *7th Annual Conference on Health, Inference, and Learning*, which took place in Seattle at the end of June. The 60-degree temps were a welcome respite from the soaking, humid 95-degree environs of Durham, NC. I went with three good friends from school, which always makes a conference better. This was my first time at CHIL.

The conference was officially two days, Monday and Tuesday, but it was preceded by an event called *Health AI Builders 2.0: A CHIL Unconference*, a full day focused on bringing builders and researchers together to discuss how to build AI in healthcare. This was pretty good, since as a PhD student working in the AI for healthcare space, it's been challenging to think about how my research could one day be deployed into an application that people actually use. There are many layers in the complicated healthcare system, and they're inherently interdisciplinary — governance, legal, cybersecurity, the list goes on. A theme across the day was that **the biggest barriers are people and processes, not technology**. My favorite part was a session where builders presented their company ideas — at any stage, from conception to funded — and a roundtable probed the validity of each one. It made the abstract problems concrete in terms of deployment, and at least at my table, revealed again that just because you build an AI model that does something cool, interesting, and/or helpful, the incentive structure of healthcare and the individuals inside it may not allow that solution to thrive.

The main conference kicked off on Monday. This is a **small-to-medium, one-track conference**, which is my favorite kind of format. It was great for networking, since everyone is interested in similar topics and you get to see the same folks repeatedly. Each day was composed of keynote speakers, panels, poster sessions, and research roundtables. There was a diverse array of topics represented — wearables, diagnostics, agentic workflows, mental health. The research roundtables, where each table has a specific topic to discuss, were a highlight. I was at a table discussing **why so much AI for healthcare research never gets deployed**. Someone made the point that most researchers don't have access to actual hospitals and end up using datasets like MIMIC — that was an illuminating point. The field really needs better datasets available for everyone to work with; it's for the benefit of all of us.

Overall, the conference was great for networking and learning, and Seattle is a lively city. I'd recommend it. While agents/LLMs were a large and unavoidable theme, I was happy with the diversity of work presented.
